When alkyl halides react with silver carbonate, a nucleophilic substitution reaction occurs, resulting in the formation of **dialkyl carbonates** and **silver halide** as a precipitate. The reaction proceeds as follows:

### General Reaction:
$$
2R-X + Ag_2CO_3 \rightarrow 2AgX + (RO)_2CO
$$
Where:
- $ R-X $ represents an alkyl halide (e.g., ethyl iodide, $ C_2H_5I $).
- $ Ag_2CO_3 $ is silver carbonate.
- $ (RO)_2CO $ is a dialkyl carbonate (e.g., diethyl carbonate, $ (C_2H_5O)_2CO $).
- $ AgX $ is the corresponding silver halide (e.g., $ AgI $).

---

### Key Mechanism and Features:
1. **Nucleophilic Substitution (SN2):**  
   The carbonate ion ($ CO_3^{2-} $) acts as a nucleophile, attacking the electrophilic carbon of the alkyl halide. Silver ions ($ Ag^+ $) coordinate with the halide ion ($ X^- $), enhancing its leaving group ability and stabilizing the transition state.

2. **Dialkyl Carbonate Formation:**  
   Two molecules of alkyl halide are required to form one dialkyl carbonate. Each alkyl group ($ R $) attaches to an oxygen atom of the carbonate, resulting in a structure like $ O=C(OR)_2 $.

3. **Precipitation of Silver Halide:**  
   The silver halide ($ AgX $) precipitates out of the solution, driving the reaction forward by removing the product from the equilibrium.

4. **Reactivity Considerations:**  
   - **Primary alkyl halides** are preferred due to their favorable SN2 reactivity. Tertiary alkyl halides are less reactive or may undergo elimination instead.
   - **Iodides** are more reactive than bromides or chlorides due to their superior leaving group ability.

5. **Reaction Conditions:**  
   Polar aprotic solvents (e.g., acetone, DMF) are typically used to facilitate SN2 mechanisms. Controlled conditions minimize side reactions, such as decomposition of the carbonate.

---

### Example:
Using ethyl iodide ($ C_2H_5I $) and silver carbonate ($ Ag_2CO_3 $):
$$
2C_2H_5I + Ag_2CO_3 \rightarrow 2AgI + (C_2H_5O)_2CO
$$
This produces diethyl carbonate ($ (C_2H_5O)_2CO $) and silver iodide ($ AgI $) precipitate.

---

### Applications and Significance:
This reaction is a classic method for synthesizing dialkyl carbonates, which are valuable in organic synthesis (e.g., as ester precursors or solvents). The use of silver carbonate leverages its ability to activate halides, a strategy common in coupling reactions and halogen exchange processes (e.g., Finkelstein reaction).

**Summary:**  
Alkyl halides react with silver carbonate to form dialkyl carbonates and silver halide via an SN2 mechanism, driven by the nucleophilic attack of the carbonate ion and the precipitation of the silver halide.
