3. If a cell **cannot reach** any `1`, its distance should be -1.
4. Obviously, the distance for a `1` cell is 0; the distance for an `X` cell is also -1.

0100
1XXX

X010

Please compute sum(LCM(i, j)) for all pairs (i, j) such that 1 ≤ i ≤ 2 and 1 ≤ j ≤ 2. Here, LCM(i, j) denotes the **least common multiple** of integers i and j.

**Output Format:** Your final answer should be a single integer — the sum of LCM(i, j) over all such pairs.

--- [assistant] ---

We list all pairs (i, j) with 1 ≤ i ≤ 2 and 1 ≤ j ≤ 2:
(1,1), (1,2), (2,1), (2,2)

Compute LCM for each:
LCM(1,1) = 1
LCM(1,2) = 2
LCM(2,1) = 2
LCM(2,2) = 2

Now sum them:
1 + 2 + 2 + 2 = 7

You are given two arrays A and B of length 14. Initially:
- A = A[0]=11 A[1]=4 A[2]=8 A[3]=2 A[4]=9 A[5]=2 A[6]=7 A[7]=14 A[8]=12 A[9]=3 A[10]=6 A[11]=13 A[12]=5 A[13]=1
- B = B[0]=14 B[1]=10 B[2]=11 B[3]=10 B[4]=3 B[5]=9 B[6]=1 B[7]=12 B[8]=4 B[9]=7 B[10]=5 B[11]=13 B[12]=8 B[13]=6

Your task is to find the **minimum number of indices** i₁, i₂, ..., iₖ such that, after swapping A[i₁] with B[i₁], A[i₂] with B[i₂], ..., A[iₖ] with B[iₖ], both A and B contain **no duplicate elements**. Please output a single line containing the indices i₁, ..., iₖ, separated by spaces.

--- [assistant] ---

First, identify duplicates in each array:
- A contains “2” twice at indices 3 and 5, and is missing “10.”
- B contains “10” twice at indices 1 and 3, and is missing “2.”

We need to swap at index i where A[i]=2 and B[i]=10, so that A loses a 2 and gains a 10, and B loses a 10 and gains a 2, fixing both arrays’ duplicates at once.

Check positions with A[i]=2:
- i=3: A[3]=2, B[3]=10 → swapping gives A[3]=10, B[3]=2.  Then A has one 2 and one 10; B has one 2 and one 10, and no new duplicates arise.
- i=5: B[5]=9, so swapping would create duplicate 9 in A; that fails.

Thus a single swap at index 3 suffices, which is minimal.

Given an array A of length 9:
9 0 6 7 9 0 3 7 7

How many nonempty contiguous subarrays have a mean greater than or equal to 4?

**Output Format:** Your final answer should be a single integer — the total number of nonempty subarrays of A whose mean is greater than or equal to 4.

--- [assistant] ---

We want the number of contiguous subarrays of A whose mean ≥4.  Equivalently, for a subarray of length ℓ and sum S, we need S/ℓ ≥4 ⇔ S − 4ℓ ≥0.

Define b[i] = A[i] − 4.  Then the subarray sum S for A corresponds to the sum of b over that subarray, and we need that sum ≥0.

1. Form b:
   A = [9,0,6,7,9,0,3,7,7]
   b = [9−4, 0−4, 6−4, 7−4, 9−4, 0−4, 3−4, 7−4, 7−4]
     = [5, −4, 2, 3, 5, −4, −1, 3, 3]

2. Compute prefix sums P of b, with P[0]=0 and P[k]=b[0]+…+b[k−1]:
   P = [0, 5, 1, 3, 6, 11, 7, 6, 9, 12]

3. A subarray from i to j−1 has sum ≥0 exactly when P[j]−P[i] ≥0 ⇔ P[j] ≥ P[i].  
   So we count pairs 0≤i<j≤9 with P[i] ≤ P[j].

4. For each j from 1 to 9, count how many i<j satisfy P[i] ≤ P[j]:
   j=1: count=1  
   j=2: count=1  
   j=3: count=2  
   j=4: count=4  
   j=5: count=5  
   j=6: count=5  
   j=7: count=5  
   j=8: count=7  
   j=9: count=9  

   Summing gives 1+1+2+4+5+5+5+7+9 = 39.

5. (Check: total subarrays = 9·10/2=45, and one can verify there are exactly 6 with negative b‐sum, so 45−6=39.)

Therefore the answer is 39.

Answer: 39
